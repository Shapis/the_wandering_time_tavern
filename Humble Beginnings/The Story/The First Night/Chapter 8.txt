The storm had swallowed the city of Shadowforge whole. Rain fell in sheets, hammering rooftops and flooding the gutters until the streets ran like rivers in the dark. Beyond the walls, the road turned to mud, and the mud turned to something worse. Through it all moved two figures—one large and hunched against the wind, the other half a step behind, hood drawn low against the downpour.

"She went east," Torvald said, raising his voice over the gale. "From the tavern. The tracker's eye doesn't lie."

"You keep saying that." Vesper brushed wet hair from his eyes. "You also said the tracker's eye worked in daylight. It's nearly midnight and we're chasing a dying woman through a storm."

"And whose fault is that?" The old soldier grunted, splashing through a flooded crossing. "You're the one who promised the village elder we'd see her safe. Courier, he calls himself. Doesn't deliver letters, apparently. Delivers trouble."

Vesper didn't argue. The truth was heavier than the rain. They had watched the elf leave the Wandering Time Tavern—barely walking, pressing a hand to a shoulder that glowed with a light no wound should carry. He had seen poison like that only once before, years ago, and the man it killed had been weeping for mercy before dawn. Whatever she was—a mercenary, a killer, if the whispers in the tavern meant anything—no one deserved that death.

"There." Torvald stopped, pointing through the curtain of rain. Past Shadowforge's crumbling eastern gate, where the road climbed toward the rocky hills, the hillside split in a dark seam. A shallow cave mouth, half-hidden behind a screen of dripping pines. Water ran in a slow sheet down the rock before it, pooling at its entrance like a moat.

Vesper was moving before the words left his mouth. He scrambled up the muddy slope and ducked inside.

The entrance was small—barely more than a hollow worn into the hillside—but its stone walls held back the wind, and the floor was dry. She lay curled on her side near the back, wrapped in shadow.

She was so still. Her breathing was barely there—so faint that he pressed two fingers beneath her jaw and waited, counting heartbeats that came like slow drips of water. Beside her open hand, an empty vial of crimson glass lay on the stone. The wound in her shoulder no longer pulsed. Whatever brew she had swallowed had smothered the poison's light—and her with it, buried somewhere deep between sleep and death.

"She's alive," he said quietly.

Torvald ducked through the entrance behind him, and suddenly the cave felt very small. The old soldier looked down at the elf's face, unveiled now, small features slack and pale in the darkness. He said nothing for a moment.

"Hibernation draught," Vesper murmured. "Old couriers' trick, carrying one of these. Slows the blood, keeps the body cold and quiet. It will keep the poison from spreading—" his voice faltered slightly, "—but only if it hasn't already reached the heart. We need a fire. Warmth, herbs. Arnica, feverfew, something to draw the wound clean."

"You're a courier."

"I'm a courier." Vesper was already unbuckling her wanderer's pack, hands moving through its contents with practiced speed. Dried moss. Clean linen. A tin of crushed green paste that smelled sharply of winter and bitterness. His fingers paused over it. "And before that, I carried things for an apothecary. It pays to know what heals a body and what kills it."

Torvald watched him work—the careful cleaning of the wound, the moss packed against torn flesh, the linen wound tight. Rain whispered against the stone above them, but inside the cave it was almost peaceful.

"She's killed men," Torvald said at last. "Word in that tavern was she leaves entire camps empty."

"I heard."

"And yet here you are, in the rain, saving her life."

Vesper tied the final knot and sat back on his heels. For a long moment, he stared at the stranger they had bled beside only hours before—this quiet, deadly creature who fought like a storm given teeth. Her face, without the mask, looked otherworldly in the dim light. Ethereal. As if something celestial had descended into the dark and chosen to sleep there, hiding among mortals.

"Everyone is someone," he said finally. "Before they decide what they are. I'd like to know which she chooses."

Torvald exhaled through his nose—a sound halfway between a laugh and a sigh—and lowered himself against the cave wall beside them, resting his morning star within easy reach.

"Then we stand watch until morning," he said. "You first."

Outside, the storm raged on. The letter rested in Vesper's coat like a heartbeat he couldn't silence, and beyond the cave mouth, Shadowforge had sunk to a faint smear of light against the hills. Inside, three unlikely souls waited out the night—two watching over one, and by morning, there would be questions that needed answering.
